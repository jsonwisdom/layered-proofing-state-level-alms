## Core thesis

American citizens need recursive protection from all AI systems that influence public truth, civic claims, law, money, identity, health, education, governance, or rights.

AI may assist. AI may not become the unchallenged authority.

```text
citizen rights > model confidence
receipts > narrative
appeal > automation
public evidence > black-box authority
```

## Recursive protection definition

Recursive protection means every AI-assisted claim, decision, recommendation, or enforcement action must be able to answer:

1. What claim was made?
2. What evidence supports it?
3. What model or system produced it?
4. What data was used?
5. What rule was applied?
6. What changed from the prior version?
7. Who can appeal it?
8. How can the citizen verify it?
9. What happens if the AI is wrong?

If the system cannot answer these questions, it is not fit for civic authority.

## ALMS citizen rule

For civic claims, AI output is always classified as one of:

```json
[
  "SUGGESTION",
  "SUMMARY",
  "HYPOTHESIS",
  "UNVERIFIED_CLAIM",
  "RECEIPT_BACKED_CLAIM",
  "REPLAYED_CLAIM",
  "ROOTED_CLAIM",
  "ANCHORED_CLAIM"
]
```

Only receipt-backed, replayed, rooted, or anchored claims may be used as public proof.

## Dirty tactics this protects against

1. AI laundering: using an AI summary to make weak evidence look official.
2. Authority laundering: treating model output as government fact.
3. Version laundering: changing a claim after the public saw it.
4. Evidence laundering: linking to a source URL without freezing bytes.
5. Appeal laundering: giving citizens a chatbot instead of a real remedy.
6. Confidence laundering: replacing proof with probability language.
7. Audit laundering: publishing a polished explanation that hides failures.
